## What this paper sets out to show

Two things. It is for trustees to judge whether it does.

**That the assistant treats people safely and with care.** Many of the people who use it are vulnerable, and some are in danger. These are the decisions most shaped around them:
- On a safety route, help comes first. Someone in danger who would rather not give a detail sees every relevant helpline, and chooses (decision 4).
- If something breaks, the person still gets help, from contacts that are open at that time of day (3.7).
- A place to start is never offered in a crisis or to anyone under 16. The crisis check always comes first (3.3).
- If what someone types as their name is a sentence rather than a name, it is not kept. A name is never sent to the AI and never used on a safety route.

**That we hold ourselves to the tests we ask of others.** Connecting local support, which also comes to this meeting, says we make local support visible and help people connect, whichever door they turn to first. We ask the services we list how they connect people on. The assistant is held to the same test:
- It offers a route on to wider help, shaped to the need, rather than ending at a list (decision 6).
- We don't guess about people, and we don't guess about services. Only what a service says in plain terms can leave it out (decision 1, 3.3).
- Not every route adds a question. Activities and immigration help already connect people on in their own way, so the assistant adds no step there (decision 6).
- The same rules apply to every organisation, and no organisation always comes first (decision 2).

**Where we are not there yet, and are working on it.**
- The quick exit on the Safety route is being designed in the week of 16 November. It must be in place before that route goes live (section 5).
- Places to start are thin in some boroughs, and some are still awaiting confirmation. We are working through the confirmations with our regional coordinator before the switch, starting where cover is thinnest.

## 1. What the assistant is, in brief

This paper is about the new assistant. The Virtual Assistant helps someone find support by asking a few short questions, most of them answered by tapping a button. It then shows up to five local services, alongside the national contacts that always apply, and a link to see everything on Street Support.

Every conversation starts with a crisis check. If someone is in danger, under 16, at risk of self-harm, fleeing abuse, has been sexually assaulted or has lost their home to fire or flood, the assistant stops and goes straight to fixed safety routes. Those routes are written by people and never by AI.

It does not give advice, decide whether someone is eligible, or act on anyone's behalf. It points people to help, and they contact the service themselves.

These eight documents are the rules it must follow. The code is built and tested against them.

---

## 2. The main decisions, in one place

Seven decisions run through the documents. They are the ones a person using the assistant would notice.

**1. If you don't tell us, we don't guess.** If someone skips a question or chooses "Prefer not to say", the assistant assumes nothing. Services that depend on that answer, such as a women-only hostel or a service for veterans, are not picked for the shortlist of five. They stay available: "See all on Street Support" still shows them, with their conditions. This is a deliberate trade-off. A person who chooses not to say may not see a service among the five that could have helped them. It stays one tap away.

**2. Up to five, with the rest one tap away.** The assistant shows up to five services, and the others stay under "See all on Street Support". Where there are more, services designed for someone in the person's situation go first, and the rest take turns, so no organisation always comes first. A service is only left out of the assistant's results altogether where it says in plain terms that it cannot help someone in the person's situation, for example a women-only service for someone who has said they are male. It stays on the wider platform.

**3. A dedicated Safety from harm pathway.** There is now a specific route on the main menu for anyone who is being harmed, controlled or exploited, whatever their circumstances. It asks what kind of harm, then points to the specialist helplines and 999.

**4. On a safety route, not saying means seeing every option.** If someone in danger would rather not give a detail, such as their gender, the assistant doesn't guess. It shows every relevant helpline, with a note of who each is for and when it is open, so they can choose.

**5. AI plays no part in choosing services.** In this assistant it is used to understand what someone types when it doesn't match a button, by working out which of the options on screen they mean. The contract also allows it to explain approved wording in plainer terms, without adding to it. From there, fixed rules written by people decide what is shown. AI never picks or orders services, never gives advice in its own words and never touches the safety routes.

**6. Connection is offered where it helps, shaped to the need.** Someone who comes for one thing can also get help with what sits behind it, but is never made to explain. For food, money, health, drugs and alcohol, and work, the assistant asks one optional question about what else is going on. Someone looking for a shower gets the results first, then one offer of further help. Activities and immigration help add no question, because of how that help already works (3.4). If someone is not sure where to start, the assistant helps them work it out there and then. It also offers the choice of talking to someone in person: at a place to start early on, or at a local housing organisation once housing is the worry.

**7. We keep counts, not people.** The assistant keeps a record of each conversation, with no name, contact details or anything the person typed. Sensitive answers, such as ethnicity, are only ever kept as totals, and small numbers are left out of reports.

## 3. Document by document

Each document follows the same shape: what it is, what changes, what a person would notice, what ratification would cover, and what could go wrong.

### 3.1 Safeguarding Contract (version 3.3): for discussion

**What it is.** The rules that protect people at risk. It sets the crisis check at the start of every conversation and the fixed safety routes. It also sets seven rules that override everything else. Examples: "if something breaks, the person must still get help", and "the assistant must not assume anything the person has not said".

**What changes**
- **A Safety from harm route**, open to everyone, on the main menu and reachable from the housing safety question. It asks what kind of harm, and shows the Modern Slavery Helpline, the Salvation Army's 24-hour line and 999. It adds nothing to the crisis check.
- **Where someone fleeing abuse prefers not to give their gender,** they see every relevant helpline, as in section 2.
- **The under-16 age check runs on the Safety route too,** as it does everywhere else.

**What a person would notice.** Someone being harmed or controlled can find help straight from the main menu. Someone fleeing abuse who would rather not give their gender sees every option, with the hours of each, and chooses.

**What ratification would cover.** The contract as a whole, including the two new routes and the principle behind them. Not the specific numbers, which are checked at source before launch.

**What could go wrong.**
- *Someone controlling the person sees the screen.* Guard: situations are described in plain words without naming the harm, help is shown first, and nothing that identifies the person is kept. A quick exit is being designed, and the Safety route does not go live without it (section 5).
- *The help for someone who doesn't give their gender is weaker than for those who do.* Guard: it uses only contacts we already hold and have checked, and 999 always comes first. The Managing Director and the technical lead confirm it in writing before launch.

### 3.2 Housing Pathway Endpoints Contract (version 1.3): for discussion

**What it is.** The fixed contacts every housing conversation shows, whatever the local results. The council's Housing Options team is always one of them.

**What changes**
- Modern slavery and trafficking go through the new Safety route, as above.
- Someone fleeing domestic abuse who doesn't give their gender still gets every housing contact that applies to all domestic abuse cases: the local domestic abuse service, the council's Housing Options and Shelter.
- Two contacts are added: the Modern Slavery & Exploitation Helpline and the Salvation Army's line.
- **A wrong number is corrected.** The document gave the same number for Galop, the LGBT+ abuse line, and ManKind, the line for men. The assistant itself already had the right number. The document now matches.

**What a person would notice.** Very little, which is the point. The housing route works as now, with the gaps above closed.

**What ratification would cover.** The contract as a whole, including the changes above. This version builds on three earlier changes still with James, so it takes effect only once those are agreed.

**What could go wrong.** *A number goes out of date.* Guard: every number is checked at its source before launch and at each review.

### 3.3 Service Matching Contract (version 3.1): for discussion

**What it is.** The rules for choosing which services a person sees. This version is written for the new V4 categories, the groups services are listed under on Street Support from the platform switch.

**What it does**
- Decisions 1 and 2 in section 2: don't guess, and up to five with the rest one tap away.
- A service is only left out altogether on four grounds: age, gender, where it operates, or an access barrier. In each case the service must say so in plain terms and the person must have answered. Anything less clear excludes nothing.
- How a service describes who it is for decides how it is treated. "Only for women" is a restriction. "Designed for young people" puts it first for someone who has said that fits. "Welcomes everyone" has no limiting effect.
- The quick route, for people who want results without the questions, shows services that are open to anyone.
- **Places to start**, the local organisations anyone can walk into or phone without a referral, are offered as a person to talk to early in someone's journey, before housing is the worry. They are offered when the person would rather talk to someone, cannot yet say what is wrong, or has named something the assistant cannot connect them on for. They are never offered in a crisis, to anyone under 16, or simply because a search found nothing nearby. Only those whose own public information clearly shows the role are offered, within the same 5 km as services. For 16 and 17 year olds, the borough's youth organisation comes first where there is one.
- **Parked:** how the assistant treats trans and non-binary answers. For now, those answers exclude nothing. People are told that services set their own criteria and that every option stays on Street Support.

**How the rules are applied.** Mechanically. The matching is ordinary code with no AI in it, and it runs the same steps every time. The same answers give the same result, apart from the turn-taking order. Every decision can be traced to a rule and to a recorded detail about the service.

The judgement sits earlier, in those recorded details: which kind of help a service offers, who it is designed for and any restrictions it states. People prepare and check them. AI helps at that stage by suggesting tags and finding the relevant wording in each listing, but a suggestion only counts once a person has confirmed it. In the West Midlands, every recorded restriction rests on a quotation from the service's own text and is checked by a named person before launch. Elsewhere, until each area is reviewed, only a plain statement in the listing itself can exclude a service.

**What a person would notice.** A shorter list that fits their situation better, with everything else one tap away.

**What ratification would cover.** The selection rules, the four grounds for leaving a service out, the trade-off in decision 1, and when a place to start is offered.

**What could go wrong.**
- *A service is wrongly left out.* Guard: only a plain statement, under one of the four grounds, can do it.
- *A recorded detail is wrong.* Because the rules are applied mechanically, a wrong detail gives the same wrong result every time until it is corrected. Guard: details are checked by people before launch, and a listing whose text changes is flagged for review.
- *Someone who declines a question misses a service meant for them.* Guard: it stays under "See all". This is the trade-off ratification would accept.
- *Thin results.* Guard: the national contacts always show, whatever the local results.
- *Street Support appears to judge organisations.* Guard: we only apply each service's own stated criteria, the same rule for every service, and the turn-taking order.
- *A place to start is offered to someone who needs more urgent help.* Guard: never in a crisis or to anyone under 16; the crisis check comes first, as now.

### 3.4 Non-Housing Pathway Endpoints Contract (version 2.1): for discussion

**What it is.** What the assistant always shows for each kind of help other than housing, and what it says when nothing local matches. It is rewritten for the new V4 categories.

**What it does.** Each category has its own minimum, and its own way of offering onward connection.
- **Health** always shows NHS 111.
- **Finance** shows Citizens Advice and Turn2us's online tools.
- **Employment** shows Jobcentre Plus and the National Careers Service.
- **Addiction** shows FRANK for drugs and the National Gambling Helpline for gambling.
- **Food, Essentials and Activities** give an honest "we couldn't find anything near you", with a link to browse Street Support, when nothing local matches. A national number would mislead there, because those services need a local referral.
- **Onward connection, shaped to the need.** Food and everyday essentials now carry a route on to wider help, as the platform switch update said they would: one optional question for food, and one offer of further help after the results for showers, laundry and similar. Money, health, drugs and alcohol, and work get the same optional question about what else is going on. Activities need none, because an activity is only listed where it already connects people on. People seeking immigration help are routed by their status, to specialist organisations that mostly provide the wider help themselves. Where nothing local fits, someone waiting for an asylum decision is given Migrant Help's helpline, and anyone else a government website for finding a regulated immigration adviser, because only a regulated adviser can give immigration advice.
- Some contacts show only where they apply. For example, a service for people with no recourse to public funds shows only where the person has said that applies.

**What a person would notice.** Honest answers, and the chance of help with what sits behind their request. Nobody is given a number that won't help them.

**What ratification would cover.** The minimum for each category, and the onward connection for each, as principles. The specific contacts are checked and kept up to date under change control.

**What could go wrong.** *A contact goes out of date.* This had already happened to two of the seven contacts in the old version: one helpline has closed, and another has a new number. Both are corrected. Guard: a check at source before launch and at each review.

### 3.5 Analytics Contract (version 2.1): for discussion

**What it is.** What the assistant records about how it is used, and what it must never record.

**What it does.** One record per conversation, with no name, contact details or anything typed. It shows which kinds of help people look for, where nothing local was found, and which routes people take. Sensitive answers, such as ethnicity, are kept only as totals, counted by council area, and small numbers are left out of reports.

**What a person would notice.** Nothing. Nothing is saved on their device, and closing the page ends the conversation.

**What ratification would cover.** What may be recorded, and the limits on it.

**What could go wrong.** *A person is identified from a small number.* Guard: no identifiers, and small numbers left out.

### 3.6 Data Minimisation and Lawful Basis Note (version 2.1): for discussion

**What it is.** Why each question is asked, the legal basis for asking it, and what is kept. Every question must change the help shown, or it isn't asked.

**What changes**
- It matches decisions 1 and 4 in section 2.
- **Sensitive questions rest on the person's consent,** given by choosing to answer a question that explains why it is asked. Examples are ethnicity, sexual orientation and past convictions. People can always choose not to.
- **It says plainly that typed text goes to our AI provider,** Anthropic, and is processed outside the UK. People are told this in the first sentence of the conversation.
- **No separate Appropriate Policy Document is needed for the assistant,** because it relies only on consent for this information. Guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) says consent does not need one.
- **It sets out the checks that must be done before launch** (section 5).

**Where the legal position comes from.** The legal basis in this note is worked from the ICO's published guidance, prepared by the Managing Director. We are not seeking separate legal advice. Before launch, the Managing Director checks the consent wording against the ICO's guidance on explicit consent. James's technical assurance covers how the assistant is built, not the legal basis.

**What a person would notice.** Each personal question says why it is asked, and can be skipped without losing the essential help.

**What ratification would cover.** The questions and their purposes, the consent basis for sensitive ones, and the checks before launch.

**What could go wrong.** *Someone types personal details into the box.* Guard: only that one message goes to the AI provider, which normally deletes it within 30 days, and people are told at the start.

### 3.7 AI Usage Boundaries Contract (version 1.0): for discussion

**What it is.** Where AI may and may not be used. AI helps understand what someone types and explains approved content. It never decides what someone sees, never gives advice in its own words, never translates safety content on the fly and never overrides a safety route.

**What changes.** What happens when the AI is unavailable. The person is told plainly and given contacts that work at that time of day:
- Shelter, with its real hours (weekdays only);
- the council;
- the council's out-of-hours line, which is the best route for a housing emergency outside office hours;
- StreetLink;
- 999;
- Samaritans, where self-harm has come up.

Every contact shows its hours. No boundary changes.

**What a person would notice.** If something goes wrong, they are not left stuck or sent to a line that is closed.

**What ratification would cover.** The contract as a whole, including the updated fallback.

**What could go wrong.** *A contact is closed when the person needs it.* Guard: hours shown, with 999 and the council's out-of-hours line always included.

### 3.8 Mid-Conversation Age Detection Design Note (version 1.3): for discussion

**What it is.** If someone says they are under 16 at any point, for example "I'm 14" or "I'm in Year 9", the assistant moves them straight to the children's safeguarding route. This holds even if they gave an older age earlier. It works on fixed rules, not AI, and it only acts on what the person actually says, never on guesses.

**What changes.** Nothing in how it works. James signed off the build in April. This version brings its references up to date and adds two tests. It comes to you with the other seven.

**What a person would notice.** A young person who says how old they are is taken to the right help, wherever they are in the conversation.

**What ratification would cover.** The design as it stands. Under the note, any change to what triggers it, the threshold it uses or how it takes over the conversation comes back to the board, however minor.

**What could go wrong.** *A young person doesn't say their age.* Guard: under 16 is the second option in the crisis check at the start. The design deliberately acts only on what is said, so it does not guess.

## 4. How the documents fit together

- **The Safeguarding Contract comes first.** Where safety is at stake, it overrides the others.
- **The Service Matching Contract** decides which services are shown. The two endpoints contracts decide which fixed contacts always show alongside them.
- **The Data Minimisation Note** decides what may be asked. **The Analytics Contract** decides what may be kept.
- **The AI Usage Boundaries Contract** keeps AI out of every decision.
- **The age note** sits under the Safeguarding Contract.

## 5. Before anything goes live

These are checks, not decisions. Each is recorded with a date and evidence.
1. **The AI provider account is held by Street Support Network,** not by an individual, so our data processing agreement with Anthropic is in Street Support Network's name.
2. The account is on Anthropic's business terms, which rule out using what people type to train its models, and meets Anthropic's requirements for services used by people under 18.
3. We record where the assistant runs, and what its hosting logs keep and for how long.
4. We confirm the protection each provider relies on for processing outside the UK.
5. The help shown to someone fleeing abuse who doesn't give their gender is confirmed in writing by the Managing Director and the technical lead.
6. Every contact is checked at its source.
7. All safety tests pass, including the new ones.
8. The West Midlands record of each service's stated restrictions is complete.
9. The Privacy Policy and the Terms and Conditions match these documents, and say how the assistant meets the ICO's Children's Code, since 16 and 17 year olds use it.
10. The consent wording is checked against the ICO's guidance on explicit consent.
11. The assistant's opening sentence is built. It says the assistant uses AI, and that what people type goes to our AI provider, Anthropic.
12. The quick exit for the Safety route is designed, reviewed for safeguarding, built and tested.
13. Places to start are offered only once the assistant reads them from the platform, and this has been tested.

The Managing Director is accountable for confirming and recording completion of these checks, supported by James's technical assurance, before authorising deployment.

**Ratification sets the rules; it does not settle what is still open.** Any outstanding matter that can be resolved within the ratified rules will be completed under change control. Any resolution that changes those rules or their safeguarding protections will return for the appropriate approval before deployment.

If anything changes in the meantime, or as the checks and open points are resolved, we will update trustees by email, and seek their approval by email where it is needed.
